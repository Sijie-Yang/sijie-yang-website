  很多项目不是死在「做不出来」，而是死在「做出来了，但没人要」。

  在构建任何事物之前——产品、服务、课程、内容、甚至一次内部分享——先确认需求，你的方向才是**有对应性的**：你在回应该存在的问题，而不是回应自己脑子里的版本。

  ## 任何行动之前，先确认需求

  需求不是「我觉得用户需要什么」，而是**目标人群在真实情境里已经表现出来的痛、愿付的成本、以及现有替代方案为何不够**。

  跳过这一步直接行动，常见后果很一致：

  - **无人问津** — 你造的是你的想象，不是他们的优先级
  - **不可持续** — 没有付费或复购，单位 economics 从第一天就不成立
  - **团队信心不足** — 每次推广都像在说服世界；数据沉默，士气跟着沉默
  - **方向反复横跳** — 没有外部锚点，只能跟着内部争论走

  这些阻力往往被误读成「执行不够」「营销不够」「功能不够」。更常见的根因是：**需求从未被确认过**。

  ## 构建时，你的想象始终是片面且有限的

  做产品的人容易高估两件事：自己对用户生活的理解，以及「再多一个功能就能解决问题」。

  你的想象有几个结构性盲区：

  - 你熟悉的是**问题的一个切面**，用户活在完整的工作流里
  - 你看到的是**愿意开口的人**，沉默的大多数不会来找你聊天
  - 你把**自己的支付意愿**投影到用户身上
  - 你把**技术可行性**误当成**商业必要性**

  因此，「明确需求」不是写一份 PRD 开头的用户故事，而是**用调研把想象替换成证据**——至少替换成可证伪的假设。

  只有需求足够清晰，你才知道什么是「正确的产品」：为谁、在什么场景、解决哪一段痛、凭什么比现状好、他们凭什么付钱。

  ## 明确需求的方式：调研目标人群

  下面是一套可重复的五步流程。不必一次做完，但**每一步都要有产出**，不能只有「聊过一些人」的感觉。

  ### 第一步：确定目标人群

  不要从「所有人」开始。写清：

  - **谁**（角色、行业、规模、地理或场景边界）
  - **在什么情境下**痛（触发事件、频率、后果）
  - **谁有决策权与预算**（使用者 ≠ 付费者）

  产出物：1–2 个优先 persona，以及「暂时不做」的人群清单——同样重要。

  ### 第二步：确定调研问题

  问题设计决定你能听到什么。好的调研问题：

  - 问**过去行为**，少问「你会不会用」
  - 问**具体例子**：上次遇到这个问题是什么时候？怎么解决的？花了多少成本？
  - 避免引导：「如果我们做 X 你会喜欢吗？」几乎总是得到礼貌的 yes

  产出物：半结构化访谈提纲或问卷（10–15 个核心问题），每个问题对应你要验证的一个假设。

  ### 第三步：接触目标人群，回收结果

  接触方式按场景选：深度访谈、现场观察、问卷、试用、预售、试点合同。原则：

  - **样本要贴近真实决策链**，不只是朋友和同事
  - **记录原话与情境**，而不只记录你的解读
  - **设定最低样本量**（例如 8–12 次访谈或 30+ 有效问卷），避免「聊了三个人就开工」

  产出物：原始记录（录音摘要、笔记、表格），带来源标签（谁、什么角色、什么场景）。

  ### 第四步：结果分析，对接产品

  分析不是「总结大家说了什么」，而是：

  - **聚类**：哪些痛反复出现？哪些是噪声？
  - **排序**：痛的程度 × 频率 × 现有替代方案的失败程度
  - **翻译**：把语言变成产品语言——功能、流程、定价、交付边界
  - **对接**：写清 MVP 必须证明什么（见上一篇：付费证据），什么明确不做

  产出物：需求优先级列表 + MVP 范围 + 仍待验证的假设清单。

  ### 第五步：产品过程中不断循环

  需求不是立项时确认一次就结束。每发布一个版本、每多一类用户、每进入一个新场景，都要回到循环：

  **人群是否仍准确？问题是否仍成立？接触方式是否仍有效？分析结论是否仍被数据支持？**

  把调研嵌入节奏：每两周或每个里程碑做一次「需求复核」，比失败后大改便宜得多。

  ## 对应性，而不是聪明

  构建之前确认需求，不是为了写更长的文档，而是为了**让行动与外部世界对齐**。

  想象可以启动项目；只有调研确认的需求，才能支撑正确的产品、可持续的模式，以及团队在真实反馈中积累的信心。

  先问：**谁的需求？什么证据？** 再动手构建。

  Many projects do not fail because they could not be built. They fail because they were built—and nobody wanted them.

  Before you build anything—a product, a service, a course, a newsletter, even an internal deck—**confirm demand**. Only then is your direction **correspondent**: you respond to a problem that exists, not to the version living in your head.

  ## Confirm demand before any action

  Demand is not "what I think users need." It is the pain target groups already show in real contexts, what they will pay (in money or attention), and why current alternatives fall short.

  Skip this step and the resistance looks familiar:

  - **No traction** — you built your imagination, not their priority
  - **No sustainability** — without payment or retention, unit economics fail from day one
  - **Eroding team confidence** — every launch feels like persuasion; quiet metrics drain morale
  - **Direction whiplash** — without an external anchor, internal debate steers the ship

  These are often blamed on execution, marketing, or missing features. More often the root cause is simple: **demand was never confirmed**.

  ## When you build, your imagination is always partial

  Builders overestimate two things: how well they understand users' lives, and how often "one more feature" fixes the problem.

  Imagination has structural blind spots:

  - You know **one slice** of the problem; users live inside full workflows
  - You hear from **people who speak up**; the silent majority will not schedule a call
  - You project **your willingness to pay** onto others
  - You confuse **technical feasibility** with **commercial necessity**

  Clarifying demand is not opening a PRD with user stories. It is **replacing imagination with evidence**—or at least with falsifiable hypotheses.

  Only then can you define the *right* product: for whom, in which context, which slice of pain, why better than the status quo, and why they would pay.

  ## How to clarify demand: research the target audience

  A repeatable five-step loop. Each step needs an artifact—not just the feeling that you "talked to some people."

  ### Step 1: Define the target audience

  Do not start with "everyone." Specify:

  - **Who** (role, industry, scale, geography or scenario boundaries)
  - **When and where** pain shows up (trigger, frequency, consequence)
  - **Who has budget and authority** (user ≠ payer)

  Output: one or two priority personas, plus an explicit **will-not-serve-yet** list.

  ### Step 2: Define research questions

  Questions determine what you hear. Strong prompts:

  - Ask about **past behavior**, not "would you use this?"
  - Ask for **concrete episodes**: When did this last happen? What did you do? What did it cost?
  - Avoid leading questions: "Would you like X?" mostly collects politeness

  Output: a semi-structured interview guide or survey (10–15 core questions), each tied to one hypothesis.

  ### Step 3: Reach the audience and collect results

  Choose methods to fit the context: interviews, observation, surveys, trials, pre-sales, pilot contracts. Principles:

  - **Sample the real decision chain**, not only friends and colleagues
  - **Record quotes and context**, not only your interpretation
  - **Set a minimum sample** (e.g. 8–12 interviews or 30+ valid responses)—avoid "three chats, then we build"

  Output: raw notes (summaries, transcripts, tables) tagged by who, role, and scenario.

  ### Step 4: Analyze and align with the product

  Analysis is not "what people said in general." It is:

  - **Cluster**: Which pains repeat? Which are noise?
  - **Rank**: severity × frequency × failure of current alternatives
  - **Translate**: from user language to product language—features, workflow, pricing, delivery boundaries
  - **Align**: what the MVP must prove (paid evidence), and what is explicitly out of scope

  Output: prioritized demand list, MVP scope, and a backlog of remaining hypotheses.

  ### Step 5: Keep looping throughout the product lifecycle

  Demand is not confirmed once at kickoff. Every release, every new segment, every new scenario requires returning to the loop:

  **Is the audience still right? Is the problem still real? Are our methods still valid? Do conclusions still hold in the data?**

  Embed rhythm: a lightweight demand review every two weeks or at each milestone costs far less than rebuilding after failure.

  ## Correspondence, not cleverness

  Confirming demand before you build is not about longer documents. It is about **aligning action with the outside world**.

  Imagination can start a project. Only researched, confirmed demand sustains the right product, a viable model, and confidence built on real feedback.

  Ask **whose demand, and what evidence?** Then build.
