  很多人谈「创业」，脑子里先出现的是产品、技术栈、品牌视觉，甚至是一个听起来很酷的名字。但商业成功的关键，从来不是「我能不能造出来」，而是「有没有人愿意为这个痛付钱，而且付得持续、付得可重复」。

  我把这条路径压缩成一句话：

  **创业 = 需求发现 × MVP 验证 × 规模化复制**

  三个环节缺一不可。跳步，就会在某个阶段暴雷。

  ## 1. 掌握需求：不是凭空建造

  最危险的起点，是「我觉得世界需要这个」。世界需要的东西很多，但**愿意掏钱、愿意反复掏钱**的需求，少得多。

  找需求时，请刻意远离「最好看的需求」——那些让你兴奋、让 pitch deck 很漂亮、让朋友点赞的需求。商业上更值钱的，往往是**最痛的需求**：时间被浪费、钱在流失、合规在逼你、客户在投诉、团队在重复劳动到崩溃。

  怎么判断「够痛」？看三个信号：

  - **已有替代方案，但用户仍不满意** — 说明痛点真实，只是现有解法不够好
  - **用户愿意为你的粗糙版本提前付费或预付时间** — 说明痛到等不及完美产品
  - **问题频繁发生、决策链短** — 说明可以较快验证，不必等三年

  需求发现的产出，不是一份商业计划书，而是一份**可验证的假设**：谁、在什么场景、为什么痛、现在怎么凑合、愿意付多少。

  ## 2. 构建 MVP：用「付费」换「证据」

  MVP 不是「功能少一点的正式版」，而是**用最小成本换取商业模型是否成立的证据**。

  证据的核心问题只有一个：**有没有人付钱？**

  不是「有没有人注册」「有没有人点赞」「有没有人说不错」。那些是兴趣信号，不是商业信号。付费 — 哪怕只是一次性的、小额的、试点合同 — 才意味着对方用钱包投票，认为你的解法比现状更值得。

  MVP 可以非常粗糙：人工服务、Notion + 表格、半自动脚本、一对一咨询包、定制化报告。关键是：

  - **交付真实价值**，而不是演示 demo
  - **明确标价**，而不是「先免费用再说」
  - **记录单位 economics**：获客成本、交付成本、毛利率、复购或续约意向

  如果 MVP 阶段拿不到付费，优先怀疑的不是「推广不够」，而是**需求不够痛、定位不够准、或价值主张不够清晰**。继续加功能，往往只是在延迟面对这个问题。

  ## 3. 获得证据之后，再谈规模化

  只有当你手里有**商业模型成功的证据**，才值得投入规模化：招人、铺渠道、做品牌、上自动化、拿融资。

  什么叫「商业模型成功证据」？至少满足其中几条：

  - 真实客户重复付费，或明确续约意向
  - 单客户 economics 算得通（收入 > 可变成本 + 合理分摊的固定成本）
  - 交付流程可描述、可培训、可部分标准化
  - 获客路径可复盘，不是纯靠个人关系一次性成交

  规模化复制，本质是把「你亲手验证过的那一单」变成「更多人能重复交付、重复销售、重复获客的系统」。没有证据就规模化，常见结果是：团队越大、burn 越快、离真相越远。

  ## 把三步连成一条链

  | 阶段 | 核心问题 | 常见误区 |
  |------|----------|----------|
  | 需求发现 | 谁最痛？痛到愿意付钱吗？ | 追逐「好看」的想法，不做用户访谈 |
  | MVP 验证 | 最小交付能否换来付费？ | 堆功能、免费用，把兴趣当需求 |
  | 规模化 | 证据成立后，如何复制？ | 还没付费就融资、招人、铺市场 |

  如果你正在考虑 Substack、咨询、SaaS、或任何 side project，不妨先问：**我现在在哪一步？** 如果在第一步，别急着做 logo；如果在第二步，别急着招人；如果在第三步，先拿出付费与复购的数字。

  商业模型不是写出来的，是**被付费验证出来的**。

  When people say "startup," they often picture the product first — the stack, the brand, the pitch deck, even a clever name. But commercial success rarely starts with "can we build it?" It starts with "will someone pay for this pain, repeatedly and predictably?"

  I compress the path into one line:

  **Entrepreneurship = demand discovery × MVP validation × scale replication**

  Skip a step, and something breaks later.

  ## 1. Master demand — don't build in a vacuum

  The riskiest starting point is "the world needs this." The world needs many things; far fewer problems come with **willing, repeat payers**.

  Deliberately avoid the *prettiest* problems — the ones that excite you, look great on slides, and get polite praise from friends. What tends to pay is the **most painful** problem: wasted time, leaking money, compliance pressure, angry customers, teams drowning in manual work.

  Three signals that pain is real:

  - **Workarounds already exist, but users are still unhappy** — the problem is real; solutions are just inadequate
  - **Users prepay or pre-commit time for a rough version** — pain is urgent enough to skip perfection
  - **The problem recurs and decisions are fast** — you can validate without a three-year cycle

  The output of demand discovery is not a business plan. It is a **testable hypothesis**: who, in which context, why it hurts, how they cope today, and what they might pay.

  ## 2. Build an MVP — trade payment for evidence

  An MVP is not "the real product with fewer features." It is the **cheapest way to learn whether the business model works**.

  The central question: **did anyone pay?**

  Not sign up. Not like. Not say "nice idea." Interest is not commerce. Payment — even once, small, or as a pilot — means someone voted with their wallet that your solution beats the status quo.

  MVPs can be ugly: manual service, Notion + spreadsheets, scripts, 1:1 consulting, bespoke reports. What matters:

  - **Deliver real value**, not a demo
  - **State a price**, not "free for now"
  - **Track unit economics**: acquisition cost, delivery cost, margin, renewal intent

  If the MVP does not get paid, the first suspect is usually not "marketing" but **weak pain, wrong segment, or unclear value**. Adding features often delays that conversation.

  ## 3. Scale only after you have proof

  Invest in scale — hiring, channels, brand, automation, fundraising — only when you hold **evidence that the model works**.

  What counts as proof?

  - Repeat payers, or clear renewal intent
  - Unit economics that work (revenue > variable cost + fair share of fixed cost)
  - A delivery process you can describe, train, and partially standardize
  - A repeatable acquisition path, not one-off favors

  Scaling is turning the deal **you** closed into a system others can sell, deliver, and acquire with. Scaling before proof tends to produce larger teams, faster burn, and slower learning.

  ## Chain the three steps

  | Stage | Core question | Common mistake |
  |-------|---------------|----------------|
  | Demand discovery | Who hurts most? Will they pay? | Chasing pretty ideas; skipping user talks |
  | MVP validation | Can minimal delivery earn payment? | Feature creep; free users mistaken for demand |
  | Scale | How to replicate after proof? | Fundraising and hiring before anyone pays |

  Whether you are launching on Substack, consulting, SaaS, or a side project, ask: **which step am I on?** At step one, skip the logo. At step two, skip hiring. At step three, show payment and retention first.

  A business model is not written. It is **validated by payment**.
